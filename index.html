Module 01 — The sentence

Say it before you name it

You do not have to know the diagnosis to sound like you know what you are doing. Derm consults are won on description. Build the sentence in order, every single time, and the differential tends to fall out of it on its own. Naming the thing is most of the power — ask any wizard.

Module 02 — Morphology

The vocabulary that does most of the work

Every lesion drawn here is a cross-section through skin: stratum corneum on top, epidermis, dermis, then fat. The reason macule versus papule matters is that it tells you which layer the disease is living in. Everybody wants the powers; nobody wants the training montage. This is the montage.

Module 05 — Shapeshifters

The ones that wear another disease's face

Some diagnoses are missed because they are rare. These are missed because they are impersonating something ordinary — and they will keep the disguise for as long as you keep treating what they are pretending to be. Every one of them has variants. Some of them are glorious.

"It's never lupus."

Except in dermatology, where it reasonably often is. House was right about internal medicine and wrong about skin. Cutaneous lupus is common, it is frequently the first and only sign, and discoid lesions scar permanently if you spend six months calling them seborrhoeic dermatitis. Photodistribution, follicular plugging and scarring alopecia are your three cues — biopsy rather than guess.

Module 06 — Skin of colour

Erythema is not always pink

Most derm atlases were photographed on Fitzpatrick I–III skin. Your clinic is mostly IV–VI. That gap is where diagnoses get missed, so it is worth learning deliberately rather than by accident.

Module 07 — Prescribing

Potency, and how much to actually give them

Two things nobody teaches properly: which steroid goes where, and the fact that a 30 g tube sent home for a whole-body eczema flare will run out in under two days. The fingertip unit fixes the second problem. With great potency comes great responsibility, mostly toward thin skin.

Vehicle matters as much as potency

Ointment — most potent, best for dry, thick, lichenified skin. Greasy, so poor adherence.
Cream — the default compromise; fine for most.
Lotion / gel / foam — hairy skin and scalp.
Wet, weeping skin takes a drying preparation, not a greasy one.

Where thin skin punishes you

Face, eyelids, genitals, and flexures absorb dramatically more. Keep to mild or moderate and time-limit it. Scrotal skin absorbs roughly forty times more hydrocortisone than the forearm — the classic teaching figure, and the reason steroid atrophy shows up there first.

Fingertip unit calculator

One FTU is the ribbon from the distal crease to the tip of an adult index finger — about 0.5 g, enough for two adult palms of skin.

A note for the intern reading this at midnight

The feeling that your dermatology is not good enough is not evidence that your dermatology is not good enough. It is evidence that you have noticed how large the field is — which is the first genuinely diagnostic skill anyone develops in it. Interns who feel fluent in derm after a few months have usually just not met enough skin yet.

Rotations are designed around exposure, not mastery. Nobody rotating through medicine expects to leave as a cardiologist. Derm gets held to a stranger standard, mostly because the findings are visible and everyone in the room feels entitled to an opinion about them.

What actually gets better, fast: describing accurately, recognising the nine emergencies above, knowing which steroid strength goes on a face, and being comfortable saying you will look it up. That is a genuinely competent intern. The other four thousand diagnoses arrive over years, and they arrive whether or not you panic about them tonight.

The ninety-second matcha rule

When the list is long and your hands have gone slightly stupid, that is a signal, not a character flaw. Leave the ward. Ninety seconds, something warm, and no clinical decision-making. Rounds will survive it, and you will come back able to actually see the next rash.
